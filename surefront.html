Making room for the first idea.

A new ideation workflow for creative teams, before a product has a complete specification.

My roleProduct Manager
Team4-person CMU industry studio
WhenJanuary–December 2024
Problem & goalUser journeyMy roleDecisionsImpact

PRODUCT MISSION

Help retailers and brands develop a product from its first spark of inspiration.

Problem & goal

Surefront’s existing workflow supported products once their specifications were taking shape. But a creative’s first idea might just be a photo, a PDF, or a slide deck.

Our goal: let teams capture inspiration and develop it into a structured product concept within Surefront.

Solution

A Trend Board where teams can upload and organize creative assets, review ideas together, and add product details as a concept develops. We delivered the upload foundation first, followed by the board workflows.

User journey

My role

I translated client goals into a prioritized roadmap, negotiated scope, and guided delivery across two releases. I worked with our four-person team to decide what to build first, clarify acceptance criteria, and incorporate feedback from demos and client testing. My engineering background also let me contribute to the backend and assess implementation trade-offs.

Discovery centered on client meetings, Figma walkthroughs, and the existing application. Comparing the designs with current behavior helped us uncover missing steps and dependencies before committing to implementation.

Decisions

Let people start with an incomplete idea.

Early concepts did not always have the fields a finished product required. We separated capturing an idea from completing its specifications, with support for media uploads, previews, and later refinement. This kept the initial workflow aligned with how creative teams actually start.

Build the upload foundation first.

The Trend Board requirements were still evolving, while asset ingestion was a clear dependency. We split the work into two releases: uploads and media processing first, then Trend Board workflows. This gave the client a usable foundation to test while we clarified the next release.

Make the scope fit the team’s capacity.

The backlog was larger than our remaining capacity. We used MoSCoW prioritization, dependencies, and delivery velocity to reduce planned scope. The trade-off was a narrower release; the benefit was a clearer commitment the team could deliver.

Impact

The new workflow let creative teams bring early ideas into Surefront before they had complete specifications. Our team delivered asset ingestion and Trend Board features across two releases, incorporating client testing and handing the work over with documentation.

The next question is whether teams reach a useful product concept faster and keep using the workflow. These are proposed follow-up KPIs.

I would validate the measurement windows against the teams’ actual planning cadence.

A little more on the engineering

I contributed to the Python/Django backend and asynchronous upload work. Moving media processing into background jobs helped keep long uploads from holding up the user’s request.

The team also built a reusable thumbnail pipeline: download, convert, resize, and upload, with converters for different file types. The final presentation reported 83% less bootstrap time and 80% less code for this approach.

Our quality plan covered upload flows, feature flags, unit tests, code review, and release checks.

What I took away

Requirements need priorities, not just detail. I learned to bring dependencies and team capacity into scope discussions earlier, keep stories small, and leave room for testing and fixes.
